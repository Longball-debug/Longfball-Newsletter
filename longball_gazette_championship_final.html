Championship Final — August 31–September 13, 2026
BACK ON TOP: CASCADES END 18-YEAR TITLE WAIT, BURY DESERT RATS IN PITCHING
Troutdale led the league wire-to-wire, won the Blue Division, and then did it again in the final that mattered most — overwhelming the Desert Rats' league-best offense with a two-week pitching staff performance that will be discussed in this league for years. Final: Cascades 935.33, Desert Rats 770.83.
By the Longball Gazette Wire Staff
League Headquarters — Sunday, September 13, 2026
The Troutdale Cascades mob the mound as the scoreboard makes it official — the franchise's first championship since 2008.

Eighteen years. That is how long the Troutdale Cascades waited between their first MLBLONGBALL championship, won in the league's inaugural 2008 season, and their second, secured Sunday night in a two-week title match that the Cascades controlled from the opening bell and never seriously relinquished. The final score reads 935.33 to 770.83, a 164.50-point verdict that flatters the competitiveness of a series the Desert Rats clawed back into twice and lost both times to the same opponent: Troutdale's pitching staff.

This was, on paper, the matchup MLBLONGBALL had been building toward all season. Troutdale finished the year ranked No. 1 and won the Blue Division outright. The Desert Rats finished directly behind them, division runners-up, and arrived at the championship with a lineup that had punished pitching all year. For thirteen days, that lineup did exactly what it was built to do — the Rats out-hit the Cascades 518.50 to 434.00, an 84.50-point offensive advantage built on more hits, more runs, dramatically more home runs, and more RBI. Alex Bregman alone outscored the entire Cascades bullpen. It was not enough.

Troutdale's answer was 501.33 pitching points against Desert's 252.33 — a 249-point gap nearly double the size of the Rats' hitting advantage, and more than enough to erase it twice over. Bryan Woo, the unanimous championship MVP, put up 90.50 points by himself: 20 innings, 21 strikeouts, three wins, three quality starts, a single earned run allowed. His two-start opening week alone (14 IP, 1 ER, 16 K) was, on its own, the best individual week posted by anyone in either playoff bracket this postseason. Payton Tolle, Blake Snell, Noah Cameron and Freddy Peralta backed him up in the rotation; Mason Miller and Cade Smith turned the bullpen into a second forty-point weapon. The Cascades posted a 3.01 team ERA across the series. The Rats posted 5.40.

Sunday Makes It Official

The series' final day left no doubt. Troutdale closed it out 116–72 on Sunday, winning the pitching split 79–27 even as the Desert Rats took the day's hitting battle 45–37. It was the same story that had defined the previous thirteen days in miniature: the Rats' bats did their job, and Troutdale's arms did more. The Cascades pushed the two-week score from 819–698 at the start of the day to a final 935.33–770.83, closing the book on a series in which the Rats never had their pitching staff show up when the Cascades' offense went quiet.

A Franchise-Defining Two Weeks for Woo

Strip away everything else in this series and Bryan Woo's line still stands on its own: 20 innings, 21 strikeouts, three victories, three quality starts, one earned run, a 0.45 ERA and a 0.70 WHIP over a two-week championship. His 90.50 points exceeded the Desert Rats' entire hitting advantage of 84.50 — meaning one pitcher, on his own, outproduced the full 249-point pitching gap's most direct counterargument. There is no vote to take. Woo is the Championship MVP.

Wire Intelligence — Championship Final
The Wire Goes Quiet
No waiver activity moves a championship roster in its final two weeks. Here's what that means, and what's coming once the wire reopens.

By the time a team reaches the final, its roster is its roster. Whatever moves were going to matter — the streaming pickups, the deadline waiver claims, the late-summer bullpen additions — were made weeks ago, and the two rosters that played out this championship did so with the players they'd already assembled. That's as it should be. A title decided by a $2 FAAB claim on Championship Sunday would be a strange way to end an 18-year wait.

What the two-week final did confirm is which roster construction philosophy held up under playoff pressure. The Desert Rats built around top-of-roster power — Bregman, Seager, Bellinger, McCarthy — and that group delivered almost exactly as advertised, combining for 260 points. Troutdale built pitching depth deliberately, five starters plus a three-man bullpen core all capable of double-digit points on a given day, and that depth is what actually decided the series. Neither team won or lost this because of a waiver-wire secret weapon. They won and lost because of decisions made months ago.

Receipts
By Duke Falcone  ·  The Longball Gazette

Here's the whole series in one sentence: the Desert Rats won the part of the game everyone watches and lost the part that actually decides championships. Hitting, 518.50 to 434.00, Rats by 84.50. Pitching, 501.33 to 252.33, Cascades by 249. Do that math and the pitching gap isn't just bigger — it's nearly three times bigger. You don't win a fantasy title by winning the fun category. You win it by not getting run over in the other one. The Rats got run over.

I want receipts, so here are receipts. Bryan Woo: 90.50 points, 20 innings, 21 strikeouts, three wins, one earned run allowed across the entire series. Payton Tolle: 65.33. Blake Snell: 52.33. Noah Cameron: 47. Freddy Peralta: 39.50. That's five starters averaging almost 59 points apiece, and I haven't even gotten to Mason Miller's 53.50 out of the bullpen. Add it up and Woo-Tolle-Snell-Cameron-Peralta alone — five guys — beat the Rats' entire pitching staff by more than 42 points. Entire staff. Not their worst five arms. All of them.

Now the other side of the ledger. Jakob Junis: minus-20.50. Adrian Morejon: minus-9.67. Logan Webb: 4.50 points on 16 earned runs allowed in 13 innings. That's not bad luck, that's a rotation that couldn't get outs when it mattered against a team that never stopped getting them. Bregman went off. Seager went off. McCarthy and Bellinger went off. Didn't matter. You can't out-hit a 3.01 team ERA when yours is 5.40.

Give Troutdale full credit here, because this wasn't luck stacked on luck. This is a franchise that finished No. 1 in the league all season, won the Blue Division running away, and then went out and beat the team chasing it hardest using the exact same formula that got them there: pitching depth so deep it doesn't have a hole in it. Eleven hitters over 30 points is nice. It's not why they won. They won because five different starters and a shutdown bullpen core made sure the Rats' 84-point hitting edge never had a chance to matter.

Eighteen years since the first Cascades title. I wasn't covering this league in 2008, but I don't need to have been there to tell you that kind of wait changes how a franchise thinks about itself. This year it stopped being a story about waiting and started being a story about how they finally did it — not with one signature ace, but with five guys who all showed up when it counted. That's a formula other franchises should be taking notes on this offseason. Starting, probably, with the team that just lost to it.

Eighteen Years, One Number
By Marty Aldrich  ·  The Longball Gazette

Somewhere in the record books of this league there is a line from 2008 — the inaugural season, the first champion this league ever crowned — and it says Troutdale Cascades. For eighteen years that line sat there alone, a first chapter with no second one, while other franchises rose and won titles and the Cascades did what good franchises do when they're not winning: they stayed competitive, they stayed relevant, and they waited for a season like this one.

There is something almost unfair about how clean this year's version of the story turned out. No. 1 ranking wire to wire. The Blue Division crown. And then, in the final that mattered most, a matchup against the only team that had chased them all season without ever quite catching them — their own division runner-up, the Desert Rats. Sports doesn't usually hand you a narrative this tidy. This year it did, and Troutdale finished the job.

I keep coming back to Bryan Woo's line, not because the numbers are staggering — though they are, 90.50 points and a 0.45 ERA across two weeks is the kind of stretch a pitcher remembers for the rest of his career, real or fantastic — but because of what it represents. A franchise that waited eighteen years for a second championship didn't get it from a single miracle month. They got it from a pitcher who simply refused to have a bad start when his team needed him not to. That is what patience looks like when it finally pays off.

And I don't want to let the Desert Rats' half of this story go unwritten, because there is real substance in a loss like this one. Alex Bregman's 73 points, five home runs, seventeen hits in forty-six at-bats, three strikeouts in two weeks of playoff baseball — that is a career-caliber stretch from a player on a team that came up short. Jake McCarthy, Cody Bellinger, Corey Seager, all of them delivered exactly what a contender needs from its lineup. The Rats did not lose this because their plan failed. They lost because someone else's plan, on the same field, over the same two weeks, was simply built to survive a bad night, and theirs wasn't.

Eighteen years is a long time to carry a single number in a franchise's history. Tonight, Troutdale gets to add a second one next to it. Somewhere down the road, in some future September, another team in this league is going to be carrying its own long wait, hoping for a season that ends the way this one did for Troutdale. That's what makes a moment like this worth writing down.

Big Lou's Corner: The Rats' Pitching Staff Owes Everybody an Apology
By Big Lou Palmieri, Baseball Lifer  ·  The Longball Gazette

FIVE HUNDRED AND ONE POINT THREE THREE. That's what Troutdale's pitching staff put up over two weeks and I need everybody to sit with that number because I have been sitting with it since Sunday night and I still don't fully believe it. Woo. Tolle. Snell. Cameron. Peralta. Miller. Smith. Bednar. Eight names, one staff, five hundred and one points, and a team ERA of THREE POINT OH ONE in a two-week fantasy playoff. I've seen good pitching staffs in this league. I have never seen one do that on this stage with the title on the line.

Meanwhile — and I say this as someone who genuinely likes the Desert Rats, genuinely believes in this roster, and is going to say it anyway because it needs saying — Jakob Junis finished the series at MINUS TWENTY POINT FIVE. Not zero. Not a rough week. NEGATIVE. Adrian Morejon: minus nine point six seven. Logan Webb allowed SIXTEEN earned runs in THIRTEEN innings and somehow that only cost the Rats four and a half points, which tells you how bad the rest of the staff was that Webb's disaster start barely moved the needle. This isn't a pitching staff. This is a group of guys who agreed to show up and see what happens.

And through ALL of that — through Junis, through Morejon, through Webb — Alex Bregman put up SEVENTY-THREE POINTS. Seventeen hits. Five home runs. Eleven RBI. THREE strikeouts in two weeks of championship baseball. Bregman did his job so completely, so thoroughly, that he nearly dragged an entire broken pitching staff to a championship by himself. He didn't get there. Nobody was getting there with that pitching. But I want it on the record: Bregman showed up. The rotation did not.

Now let's talk about BRYAN WOO because somebody has to and it might as well be me. Ninety point five points. TWENTY innings. TWENTY-ONE strikeouts. THREE wins. ONE earned run allowed in the ENTIRE championship. A zero-point-four-five ERA.
